# Crisis Communication and Stakeholder Management During a Suspected Ransomware Incident

## Project Overview

This project focused on how an organisation should communicate and manage stakeholders during a suspected ransomware incident that disrupts access to its shared file system.

The scenario involved a critical file-sharing service becoming unavailable, leaving several teams unable to access important documents. Although ransomware was suspected, the investigation had not yet confirmed the cause, the extent of the incident or the possible point of entry.

There were also concerns about possible disruption to the organisation's Friday payroll activities and an unverified screenshot circulating among employees.

My role in this exercise was to act as the Communications Lead, supporting the Incident Commander in coordinating clear, timely and accurate communication throughout the initial incident response period.

The exercise helped me understand that incident response is not only about resolving the technical problem. How an organisation communicates with employees, management, customers and other stakeholders can also influence the level of disruption and trust during a crisis.

## Project Objectives

The main objectives were to:

- Develop a practical communication plan for the first response period.
- Identify the key stakeholders affected by the incident and their information needs.
- Prepare an employee holding statement that communicates the situation without creating unnecessary panic.
- Draft an appropriate update for customers and business partners.
- Prepare an executive briefing highlighting confirmed facts, outstanding questions, risks and decisions required.
- Develop alternative communication channels in case normal communication systems become unavailable.
- Explain when and why an incident should be escalated.
- Reflect on the importance of balancing speed, accuracy and transparency during a crisis.

## Understanding the Incident and Its Risks

One of the first things I considered was the difference between what was known and what was still being investigated.

It would have been inappropriate to announce that the organisation had suffered a confirmed ransomware attack when the security team had not established that fact.

I therefore treated ransomware as a suspicion rather than a confirmed finding.

The main risks identified in the scenario included:

| Risk area | Potential impact |
|---|---|
| Operational disruption | Employees may be unable to access files required for daily activities. |
| Payroll continuity | The Friday payroll process could be affected if access is not restored. |
| Misinformation | Unverified screenshots may create confusion and unnecessary concern. |
| Information security | Unapproved workarounds could expose sensitive information. |
| Regulatory and legal exposure | Poorly managed communication could create additional compliance concerns. |
| Reputation | Delayed or misleading communication could affect stakeholder confidence. |

## Work Completed

### 1. Incident Communication Plan

I developed a communication plan covering the initial response period from 08:30 to 12:30 on Monday.

The plan established communication priorities, responsible persons, proposed communication times and the need for regular updates.

The objective was to ensure that stakeholders received relevant information without disclosing details that had not been verified by the investigation team.

### 2. Stakeholder Identification and Management

I identified six major stakeholder groups:

- Employees and affected business units.
- Executive management and the Board.
- Customers and business partners.
- Regulators, legal and compliance teams.
- Banking and payroll partners.
- Incident response and technology teams.

For each group, I considered the information they would need, who should communicate with them and how frequently updates should be provided.

This helped demonstrate that the same message should not necessarily be sent to every stakeholder. Employees may need practical instructions, while executive management needs information about business impact, risks and decisions requiring approval.

### 3. Employee Holding Statement

I prepared an employee communication acknowledging the disruption and explaining that the technical team was investigating the issue.

The statement also addressed the unverified screenshot circulating among employees. Staff were advised not to forward unconfirmed information, speculate about the cause or use unauthorised methods to access organisational files.

I included the payroll concern carefully. Rather than promising that salaries would be processed without delay, the communication acknowledged the potential impact and committed to providing another update.

### 4. Customer and Business Partner Communication

I drafted a separate update for customers and business partners.

The message focused on the possibility of service disruption, the organisation's ongoing investigation and the importance of using approved communication channels.

I avoided including technical details that had not been confirmed or making assurances about recovery timelines.

### 5. Executive Incident Briefing

I prepared an executive briefing structured around five areas:

- Confirmed facts.
- Information still being investigated.
- Potential business and operational risks.
- Decisions and support required from management.
- The next scheduled communication update.

This structure was useful because it allowed management to distinguish between established information and matters that still required investigation.

### 6. Communication Channel Resilience

I also considered what would happen if the organisation's usual communication systems became unavailable.

The plan identified proposed primary and alternative communication channels, together with responsible owners and a method of confirming that messages had been received.

An important consideration was that alternative channels must be approved and sufficiently independent of the affected environment. The organisation should not introduce another security risk while trying to maintain communication.

### 7. Escalation Rationale and Critical Reflection

The assessment included an escalation rationale explaining why certain developments should be brought to the attention of the Incident Commander and senior management.

These included possible payroll disruption, an expanding operational impact, evidence of data exposure, regulatory concerns and the spread of misinformation.

I also reflected on the challenge of communicating quickly without making statements that could later prove inaccurate.

One important lesson was that stakeholders need reassurance, but reassurance should come from honest updates, clear responsibilities and practical next steps rather than promises that the organisation cannot yet guarantee.

## Key Lessons Learned

Working through this exercise strengthened my understanding of the relationship between incident response, business continuity, stakeholder management and governance.

Some of the key lessons I took away were:

### 1. Accuracy should come before assumptions

I learned that there is a difference between a suspected incident and a confirmed incident. Even when ransomware is suspected, communication should reflect only what has been established. Sharing unverified information can create confusion, damage trust and make an already difficult situation worse.

### 2. Communication must be tailored to the audience

Different stakeholders have different concerns. Employees need clear instructions on what to do, customers need to understand any possible service impact, while senior management needs information about business risks and decisions requiring attention. A single general message may not address everyone's needs.

### 3. Regular updates help reduce uncertainty

Silence during an incident can encourage rumours and speculation. Even when there is no major development, providing a scheduled update helps stakeholders understand that the situation is being monitored. It is better to communicate that investigations are ongoing than to leave people relying on unverified information.

### 4. Business continuity should be considered early

The possible impact on Friday payroll showed me that an incident can affect business operations beyond the system that initially becomes unavailable. Organisations should identify critical business activities, understand their dependencies and consider alternative arrangements early enough to reduce disruption.

### 5. Alternative communication channels must be reliable and secure

Having a backup communication channel is useful, but it must be approved, accessible to the intended stakeholders and sufficiently independent of the affected environment. Moving communication to an unapproved platform could create additional information security risks.

### 6. Escalation should be based on impact and risk

Not every issue requires the same level of escalation. However, matters such as possible data exposure, growing operational disruption, payroll impact and regulatory concerns should receive appropriate attention from the Incident Commander and relevant management teams. Escalation should be timely and supported by available evidence.

### 7. Reassurance should not become a promise

The payroll concern was one of the most important communication challenges in this exercise. Employees naturally want to know whether their salaries will be paid as scheduled, but making a guarantee without confirmation could create a bigger problem later. I learned that it is possible to acknowledge concerns, explain what is being done and commit to a further update without promising an outcome that is not yet certain.

Overall, this exercise helped me appreciate that effective crisis communication requires preparation, sound judgement, accountability and coordination. It also reinforced the importance of bringing governance, risk management and business continuity considerations into incident response.

## Project Scope and Limitations

This was a simulated academic GRC exercise based on a suspected ransomware scenario. It was designed to demonstrate communication planning, stakeholder management and escalation considerations during the initial incident response period.

The project did not involve investigating a live ransomware attack, conducting malware analysis, performing digital forensics, implementing technical containment measures or recovering affected systems.

The communication timelines and alternative channels were proposed as part of the exercise and would require organisational approval before real-world implementation.

## Conclusion

This exercise gave me an opportunity to look beyond the technical side of a cybersecurity incident and consider how people, business operations and organisational responsibilities are affected.

I found the communication aspect particularly important because the way information is handled during an incident can either help maintain confidence or create additional uncertainty.

It also reinforced my interest in Technology Risk and Cyber GRC, particularly in areas where risk management, internal controls, business continuity and information security come together.

## Disclaimer

This project is a simulated academic exercise. It does not represent an actual ransomware incident or the activities of a real organisation. No real customer information, confidential organisational records or production systems were used.
